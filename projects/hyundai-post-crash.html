Research-Driven Interface Design

Post-Crash UX: Multi-Modal Interfaces for Immediate Driver Response

Settling an internal design disagreement over accident-response UI with a controlled 25-driver crash-simulation experiment.

Organization Hyundai Motor Company
Role Product Designer — GUI Concept, Experiment Design, Results Analysis & Paper Writing
Recognition Co-filed USPTO patent · Work-in-Progress paper, AutomotiveUI 2025
Paper Post-Crash UX Paper (PDF)

Overview

When Hyundai's team disagreed over whether a new in-car "accident response mode" should use a step-by-step Wizard UI or a conversational Chat UI, and on which device (vehicle display vs. mobile), I designed and ran a controlled crash-simulation experiment with 25 drivers to settle the question with data instead of opinion. The study found that device and interface preference split sharply by driving experience and accident history — evidence that directly shaped a personalized-design recommendation — and the results were accepted as a Work-in-Progress paper at AutomotiveUI 2025.

Problem & Context

The team was designing a new vehicle-system concept letting drivers handle minor accidents themselves (auto-notifying police/ambulance/insurance, collecting on-site information), but ran into three blockers:

  • Split on two open design questions: an AI Chat-style interface vs. a step-by-step guided (Wizard) interface, and whether it should live on the in-vehicle display or the driver's mobile phone
  • Existing academic research on driver behavior focused almost entirely on accident prevention (risk perception, cognitive load in complex driving environments, trust in ADAS) and offered no evidence on how drivers want to be supported in the minutes immediately after a collision
  • With no reference concept and no usability research to draw on, design decisions were being made from individual team members' personal driving/accident experiences rather than evidence

Process & Approach

Between-Subjects Lab Experiment Driving-Simulator Rig NASA-TLX Chi-Square / Cramer's V Thematic Coding

Designed and ran a between-subjects lab experiment with 25 licensed drivers, screened by age, gender, education, driving experience/frequency, digital literacy, and prior accident history. Each session followed a 4-stage protocol — screening, pre-interview, experimental task, and post-interview, detailed below — and combined three analysis methods:

  • Cognitive workload was measured quantitatively with the NASA-TLX scale
  • Preference differences were tested for statistical significance using chi-square/Cramer's V
  • Open-ended interview responses were analyzed via thematic coding to surface qualitative themes

Solution & Design

Designed and prototyped two complete interface concepts, each built to run on either device form factor (in-vehicle display or mobile), so the experiment could independently test device and interface type. This 2×2 device × interface design, backed by a physical simulator rig (dashboard monitor for the crash video, a vehicle-interface display, and a mounted mobile device), turned an internal design disagreement into a testable hypothesis.

Wizard UI

An explicit step-by-step flow: accident detection → automatic report confirmation (119 ambulance / 112 police / insurance company) → live collection of accident-related info (nearby CCTV, vehicle condition) → a final accident-handling status screen.

Chat UI

The same underlying accident-response logic delivered through an AI assistant conversation — asking clarifying questions (e.g., "Is an ambulance needed?"), narrating automatic-reporting progress, and surfacing the same status information conversationally rather than as discrete screens.

Impact & Outcome

Across the 25 participants, device and interface preference were driven almost entirely by driving experience and accident history, not personal taste. Overall, mobile was the more preferred device (64% vs. 36% for the in-vehicle display), but that aggregate masked the sharper experience-based split below.

Key Insights

Interface Acceptance Varies by Driver Characteristics
  • Driving experience, accident history, and trust in automation significantly influenced post-accident interface preference.
  • Even for the same system, perceived usefulness, reliability, and convenience varied by user experience level and accident history.
Strengths of Wizard UI (Step-by-Step Guide)
  • Advantageous for novice drivers.
  • Clear procedural guidance and simple visual composition let users follow along even in a confusing accident situation.

"In a shocking moment, simple and clear screens help me stay focused."— P27

Strengths of Chat UI (Conversational Interface)
  • Preferred by experienced drivers and those with a prior accident history.
  • Conversational interaction provided psychological comfort, and automated-reporting features improved efficiency.
  • Transparently surfacing insurance-filing and emergency-rescue progress increased users' sense of control.

"Seeing the process in real time made me feel reassured and in control."— P09

User Perception Gap: Trust vs. Anxiety Toward Automation
  • Some users felt anxious that no human was involved in the automated process — e.g., resistance to the system handling things unilaterally without direct communication with the other driver.
  • UX design needs to increase automation's "transparency" and "sense of participation."

"It felt less trustworthy when humans weren't involved at all."— P23

The study was accepted as a Work-in-Progress paper at AutomotiveUI 2025 ("Post-Crash UX: Multi-Modal Interfaces for Immediate Driver Response"), and the underlying interface concepts were developed into a patent co-filed with the USPTO.

Reflection

This project shows how a team-level design disagreement with no clear "right answer" can be resolved by designing a controlled experiment rather than continuing to debate from anecdote. Segmenting the statistical results by driving experience and accident history — rather than only reporting an aggregate preference — turned a simple "which UI is better?" question into an actionable, personalized-design recommendation (guide novices step-by-step; let experienced drivers delegate to automation).
